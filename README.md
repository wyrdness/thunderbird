## Overview

The Thunderbird is a powerful supernatural being widely depicted by Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast, Great Lakes, Great Plains, American Southwest, and US East Coast. In Algonquian, Ojibwe, Menominee, Ho-Chunk, and other traditions, it generates thunder by flapping its wings and lightning by flashing its eyes, and is often portrayed in opposition to underwater serpents or 'horned' panthers. Archaeological evidence places thunderbird depictions in North America at least 4,000 years before present, with petroglyphs near Twin Bluffs, Wisconsin attributed to a shelter used circa 250 BCE to 1500 CE. In modern cryptozoology, 'Thunderbird' has been adopted to describe reports of giant birds with wingspans of 4.5–6 m (15–20+ ft), most famously in the 1977 Lawndale, Illinois case in which a 10-year-old boy was briefly lifted off the ground, and in a wave of Pennsylvania sightings investigated by researcher Stan Gordon in 2001. Mainstream science treats the cryptozoological 'Thunderbird' as misidentification of large extant raptors (golden eagles, vultures, condors), while the Indigenous Thunderbird remains a living cultural and spiritual presence distinct from the cryptid framing.

- **Origin**: English calque of various Indigenous terms (e.g. Lakota Wakįnyą, Ojibwe Animikii) describing a supernatural bird that produces thunder. The cryptozoological usage was popularized in the 20th century by writers including Ivan T. Sanderson and John A. Keel.
- **Meaning**: Bird whose flight, eyes, or voice produce thunder and lightning
- **First use**: English term in widespread anthropological use by the late 19th century

## Notable Sightings

### Lawndale, Illinois — Marlon Lowe incident ⭐
> **Date**: 1977-07-25  
> **Location**: The Lowe family yard, Lawndale, Logan County, Illinois  
> **Witnesses**: 7 (Mother (Ruth Lowe), 10-year-old child (Marlon Lowe), Family and neighbors)  
> **Evidence**: Witness testimony; Contemporary local press coverage  
On the evening of 25 July 1977, Ruth Lowe heard a piercing scream from her 10-year-old son Marlon. According to Ruth and other witnesses, two very large dark birds were chasing Marlon; the larger sank its claws into his shirt and briefly lifted him (Marlon weighed ~56 lb) before dropping him after Ruth attacked the bird. Ruth described the bird as having a white ring around a half-foot-long neck, a very black body, and a six-inch hooked bill.
**Credibility**: medium — Most-cited modern Thunderbird case.
**Sources**: Coleman, L., Mysterious America; Atlas Obscura coverage of the Lawndale Thunderbirds

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Living spirit-being (Indigenous traditions)** _(Indigenous knowledge keepers across many nations)_

In many Indigenous traditions, the Thunderbird is a real, living spirit-being that controls weather, polices moral order, and engages in cosmological combat with underworld serpents. This is distinct from cryptozoological framing.

### Scientific

**Surviving Teratornithidae** _(Mark A. Hall)_

Hypothesis that giant-bird sightings represent a relict population of teratorns (e.g. Teratornis merriami, Aiolornis incredibilis), large North American birds known from Pleistocene fossils.

**Pterosaur-fossil influence on tradition** _(Adrienne Mayor)_

Some Indigenous thunderbird stories may have been influenced by Native discoveries of pterosaur fossils in western North America.

### Skeptical

**Misidentification of large extant birds** _(Sharon A. Hill, Benjamin Radford)_

Reports represent misidentification of golden eagles, turkey vultures, sandhill cranes, herons, or escaped condors - whose true wingspans are routinely overestimated by startled observers.

**Cultural tall tale** _(Joe Nickell)_

Modern 'giant bird' wave is a cultural complex amplified by media and folklore, with no specimen or unambiguous evidence.


## Indigenous Perspectives

> ⚠️ **The Indigenous Thunderbird is a culturally living spiritual entity and is distinct from the cryptozoological 'giant bird' framing. Conflating the two erases this distinction.**

**Cultures**: Kwakwaka'wakw, Haida, Tlingit, Coast Salish, Ojibwe (Anishinaabe), Menominee, Ho-Chunk, Lakota, Lenape, Iroquois Confederacy

Among the most widespread sacred figures in North American Indigenous religion; central to crests, ceremonies, songs, and origin narratives.
